The premise

What is MetricCage?

MetricCage does not hand you a name. It hands you a number that means what it says.

That distinction is the whole design. Most fight analysis collapses into a binary pick, which is right or wrong and tells you nothing about how sure anyone was. MMA generates enough noise that binary scoring is close to useless: a perfectly timed counter ends a fight in nine seconds regardless of who prepared better, and under a right-or-wrong scoreboard, being correct about the shape of a fight looks identical to guessing.

So the model is built and judged on calibration. When it says 69 per cent, the fighter should win about 69 per cent of the time. That is a harder standard than accuracy and a more useful one, because a calibrated number can be acted on and a pick cannot.

The same machinery runs in a second direction. To price a fight the model has to measure the sport (what styles exist, which ones finish fights, how long fights last, where submissions come from) across sixteen years of data. Those measurements are findings in their own right, and they show an art that is visibly changing: fights are lasting longer, submissions are disappearing, and the points fighter is winning the evolutionary argument.

The goal is not to be right more often. It is to be right about how often you will be right.

Across the 2026 season the winner head scored an AUC of 0.691 on fights it had never seen, and on the 55 occasions it committed to a fighter at 65 per cent or better, that fighter won 42 times. Seventy-six per cent, with no betting-market information of any kind.

The round distribution is worth being blunt about.

It is a hard-coded prior of 40, 30, 15, 10 and 5 per cent across rounds one to five, identical in every fight the model has ever scored. It reflects how UFC finishes are historically distributed and carries no information about the specific fight in front of it. Anywhere a round appears on this site, read it as a base rate rather than a forecast.

It also produces the features driving each call, and warnings when the four internal models disagree, when a fighter carries a recent knockout loss, or when there is too little history to say anything at all.

Sequences

How it predicts sequences in fights

A fight is a chain of questions, and MetricCage answers them separately before multiplying them back together into a shape.

The first question is who wins. That is the ensemble, and it is the part of the system that works.

The second is how. This runs through a two-stage classifier: stage one asks whether the fight gets finished at all, and only if it does does stage two ask whether the finish is a knockout or a submission. Combining the two gives six outcomes: either fighter, by any of three methods.

That six-way tree is the object this site keeps returning to, because it is where a probability model says something a pick cannot. Take Vallejos against Emmett, a five-round main event in March. The model put Vallejos at 69.6 per cent and then split that across methods with the knockout branch on top, unusual because in championship-distance fights the decision branch is normally the safe bet. Vallejos knocked Emmett out in the first round. The pick was right and so was the shape, and the shape is what a favourite-backing heuristic cannot produce.

A winner is a coin. A shape is a claim about how the fight actually goes.

An earlier draft ran the tree to a third level and reported how often the model named the round. That was wrong and it has been removed: the round distribution is constant, so any apparent skill at calling the round was the base rate doing the work. The tree stops at two levels: fighter and method.

Architecture

Four models that disagree on purpose

There is no single model. Each stream produces its own win probability, and the four are blended with weights fitted on a 2023 validation slice by minimising log loss, not chosen by hand.

The blend then passes through two corrections: an isotonic calibrator that maps raw output onto observed frequencies, and a temperature adjustment that pulls extreme calls back toward the middle. Raw ensembles are systematically overconfident, and an overconfident probability is worse than no probability at all.

Gradient boosting: XGBoost over 67 pruned features, catches interactions35.3%
Logistic regression: all 102 features, stable and sane on thin data29.2%
Career LSTM: reads each career in order, spotting whether a fighter is improving, plateauing, or sliding23.1%
Elo: strength of schedule, independent of any statistic12.4%

The disagreement between streams is itself a signal.

When the boosted model and the Elo rating land twenty points apart, that spread is reported as a warning rather than averaged away quietly. A wide spread usually means the matchup sits in a region where the historical data is thin or contradictory.

Results · walk-forward, no hindsight

How has it tracked so far?

Training stopped on 4 May 2025. The model was then rerun across every card from January to May 2026 in order, with ratings and histories updated only as each event finished. Fourteen events, 175 fights, no odds feature, no hindsight.

Two things need saying about that 76.4 per cent.

It is measured on 55 fights, which is a small sample, and the interval runs from 65 to 88 per cent. Even the bottom of that range beats backing every betting favourite. But the honest reading is that the model is somewhere in that band rather than precisely at 76.

AUC across all 175 fights is the measurement that matters more.

Accuracy on a filtered subset is a headline; AUC cannot be improved by choosing which fights to count. Published academic models on UFC data sit between 0.68 and 0.74, and those mostly include betting odds as a feature. This one does not. The project set itself a target of 0.74 and has not reached it, but 0.691 on a genuinely blind season is a real result rather than a hopeful one.

Beyond prediction · the research tool

How the art of MMA is changing

To price fights the model has to measure the sport, and the measurements double as a longitudinal study. Three shifts show up consistently across sixteen years of data, and all three point the same way: the sport is getting harder to finish and harder to specialise in.

The art, then and now

Four era shifts from the same feature pipeline that prices fights. Hover a panel for the reading.

2026 season report · sixteen years of UFC data, 2010 – 2026

Fights are lasting longer. The decision rate has climbed roughly seven points since the early 2010s and average fight time is up more than half a minute. Defensive standards have risen faster than offensive ones.

Submissions are disappearing. Down to 12.6 per cent of fights in 2026, the lowest in the data, and the ones that land no longer come from the fighters style data marks as grapplers.

The points fighter is winning the evolutionary argument. Its prevalence has risen almost without interruption for fifteen years, from 0.50 to 0.59, and the rising decision rate is that trend expressed in results.

The drift has a modelling consequence.

A method classifier trained across the whole history of the sport is fitting a distribution of finishes that is actively moving. The winner head is insulated, because who wins is a more stable question than how. The method head is not, and its 2026 failure is that drift showing up in the scores.

Honesty

What it does not know

Three gaps are worth stating plainly, because they explain most of the misses in the season record.

  • The fighter database contains UFC bouts only. A debutant arriving from a regional circuit has no history, and the model declines to predict rather than inventing one.
  • There is no feature for a weight-class change or a short-notice replacement. A fighter moving up a division looks identical to one who is not.
  • Nothing about the camp is visible. Injuries, a bad weight cut, a coaching change, a fighter who stopped sparring three weeks out: none of it reaches the data.

There is also no positional data of any kind.

Every ground feature in the model is inferred from archetype scores rather than observed, because the source data records takedowns and submission attempts but not where a fighter spent the round. The submission-chain family is an elaborate attempt to work around that gap, and the 2026 result suggests the workaround has limits.

These are calibrated probability estimates from a model with documented limits. Not betting advice, not a forecast of any individual result. About a third of the calls will miss. The value is in the number attached to each one.
