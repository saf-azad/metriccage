What a blind season taught

Discoveries

A full blind season tells you things a good season does not. Three findings, in order of how much they change the picture — followed by what the same pipeline measures about the changing art of MMA itself.

Finding one · a negative result

The method head did not work this season

AUC 0.416 · below chance

This is the most important result in the report and it is a negative one.

Stage one of the method classifier asks whether a fight reaches the judges. Across the 175 fights of 2026 its AUC was 0.416, with a bootstrap interval running from 0.330 to 0.501. An AUC of 0.5 means no information. Below 0.5 means the ranking is inverted: the fights the model flagged hardest as decisions were disproportionately the ones that got finished. Ninety-seven per cent of bootstrap samples came in below chance, so this is not a rounding artefact.

Two qualifications are due. Stage two, which asks whether a finish is a knockout or a submission, scored 0.580 across the 79 finished fights — modestly above chance. The problem is concentrated in stage one. And the model’s average distance estimate was 45.1 per cent against an actual 54.9, so part of the failure is a level error: it was trained across the whole history of the sport and still carries a finishing rate from an era that has passed. But a level error would show up as poor calibration with intact ranking. An AUC of 0.416 is a ranking failure, which is worse.

The winner head earns its place. The method head, on this season, is subtracting value rather than adding it.

Always assuming a decision would have ranked the correct outcome first on 35 per cent of fights against the model’s 27, and on high-conviction fights the gap widens to fourteen points. That is why every method estimate on this site is labelled as decoration rather than information.

Finding two

Fights that go the distance are the most predictable thing in the sport

63.5% on decisions
96 / 175 fights that reached the judges in 2026 — where the winner head does its best work

Ninety-six of the 175 fights reached the judges, and this is where the winner head does its best work: 63.5 per cent accuracy on fights that went to a decision. It is also where the base rate is most useful, since more than half of all modern UFC fights end this way.

Distance is legible because it is the absence of an event rather than an event. Two fighters with sound defence, similar pace and no obvious finishing route produce fifteen minutes of scoring, and every one of those conditions is visible in career data. A knockout depends on one shot landing in one instant, and nothing in career data predicts an instant.

The sport has been drifting this way for fifteen years: the decision rate has climbed from about 48 per cent in the early 2010s to 54.9 in 2026, with average fight time up from 10.8 minutes to 11.4.

Finding three

Surprise submissions came out of nowhere

0 of 22 ranked first

The model carries its most elaborate feature for exactly this: position distributions estimated from style, base choke probabilities by position, chain weighting, round fatigue, and a takedown gateway. Across 2015 to 2025 it worked. In 2026 the relationship inverted.

Submission rate by predicted danger quartile

Historically the top quartile nearly doubles the bottom. In 2026 the top quartile produced the fewest submissions of any group.

2026 season report · figure 10 · 22 submissions in 175 fights

The fights it flagged hardest produced the fewest submissions. Across the season, bouts that actually ended in a submission sat at the 49.6th percentile of chain danger — the median. The feature had no idea. None of the twenty-two was the model’s first-ranked outcome and only one made its top two.

This connects directly to the method-head failure above, and it is probably the same problem seen from a different angle. Submissions in 2026 ran at 12.6 per cent of all fights, the lowest in sixteen years of data and well below the 18.6 per cent average of the preceding years. They got rarer, and the ones that landed came from fighters the style data did not mark.

One season cannot settle which explanation is true.

Whether this is a one-season fluctuation on a sample of twenty-two, or the consequence of takedown defence improving to the point where the archetype-to-position mapping no longer holds, cannot be decided from a single year of data. Either way, the mapping the feature depends on has stopped describing how the ground game currently works.

The pairing generates the outcome

How styles make fights

The archetype system’s real job is not classifying fighters. It is predicting what happens when two particular kinds of fighter are locked in together. The pairing generates the outcome, not the fighter.

Two readings are worth pausing on. The Muay Thai striker, who wins by accumulated volume and body work rather than single shots, shortens fights more reliably than the power puncher does — volume beats power as a finishing mechanism. And the pressure striker slightly lengthens fights despite the reputation: forward pressure produces damage, but it also produces clinches, fence time and rounds that get scored rather than stopped.

The model encodes these interactions explicitly rather than hoping they emerge. Knockout danger runs against the specific opponent’s chin durability. Submission threat runs against their takedown defence. A counter striker’s value is computed directionally, against the other fighter’s pressure, because a counter puncher with nobody coming forward has nothing to counter. Roughly a fifth of the feature set exists for this.

The Vallejos call is that reasoning in miniature.

Emmett’s chin durability had eroded across a long career; Vallejos scores high on power puncher and pressure striker; the age-curve gap was sixteen years. Three separate feature families pointed at the same branch, which is why a knockout outranked a decision in a five-round main event. Given the method head’s season, the fair way to read it is as one good call from a component that did not work reliably across the season — the full breakdown.

The research tool · sixteen years of data

How the art of MMA is changing

Three shifts show up consistently across sixteen years of data, and all three point the same way: the sport is getting harder to finish and harder to specialise in.

Fights are lasting longer. The decision rate has climbed roughly seven points since the early 2010s and average fight time is up more than half a minute. Defensive standards have risen faster than offensive ones.

Submissions are disappearing. Down to 12.6 per cent in 2026, the lowest in the data, and the ones that land no longer come from the fighters style data marks as grapplers. Grappling-first prevalence is simultaneously at a fifteen-year low.

The points fighter is winning the evolutionary argument. Its prevalence has risen almost without interruption for fifteen years, from 0.50 to 0.59, and the rising decision rate is that trend expressed in results.

There is a modelling consequence to all of this, and the method-head finding is it.

A method classifier trained across the whole history of the sport is fitting a distribution of finishes that is actively moving. The winner head is insulated from this, because who wins is a more stable question than how. The method head is not.

A closing note

What these numbers are

These are calibrated probability estimates from a model with documented limits, running on data that stops months before the cards it predicts. Not betting advice, not a forecast of any individual result. The winner head is good and measured; the method head did not work this season and is reported as such; the round is a base rate wearing a prediction’s clothes.

About a third of the calls will miss. The value is in the number attached to each one.
