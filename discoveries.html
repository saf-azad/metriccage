Findings

Nothing below is quoted from somewhere else. Every number was worked out from scratch, using the public round-by-round record of every UFC fight since 1993. Each figure says how many fights it rests on, and each section names the test that would prove it wrong.

01 · method

Why these are rates, not percentages

Fights got longer and busier over this period, so every era figure on this page is given per minute of cage time rather than as a percentage of a total that was itself moving the whole time.

A percentage has two moving parts: the thing being measured, and the size of the pile it is a slice of. When the pile roughly triples across three decades, a slice can shrink even though the thing it describes never changed. Percentages and rates end up answering different questions, and only one of those questions is about fighting.

Measuring against the clock settles it. Across the window, significant strikes landed per fight-minute rise from 2.76 to 8.04 — that is the scale of the shift a percentage would quietly have to absorb.

How to prove this wrong

Work out significant strikes landed per fight-minute for each era. Were it flat, percentages and rates would agree and this whole precaution would be unnecessary. It runs 2.76 → 8.04, so they don’t.

Per minute of cage time, 1990s → 2020s: distance 0.86 → 6.50 · clinch 0.68 → 0.79 · ground 1.22 → 0.76. Distance striking grew more than sevenfold; the clinch is 16% busier than in 1997.

02 · sample sizes

How much evidence sits behind each era

The record is not spread evenly across the years it covers. The early 1990s contribute a few hundred rounds; the 2020s contribute tens of thousands. Showing that curve rather than hiding it means you can see for yourself how much weight any era claim is carrying.

For the same reason, every era figure on this page prints the sample behind that specific figure, not the impressive-looking total for the whole dataset.

03 · target selection

What happened to leg strikes

Leg strikes have held flat at 15–17% of everything thrown for two decades, drifting only from 0.147 to 0.162 across the full window.

The calf kick arrived and spread during exactly that period, and this is what its arrival looks like in the aggregate numbers: a swap, not an addition. Fighters changed which leg strike they were throwing without changing how often they threw one.

Worth remembering whenever a total looks reassuringly stable: it can hide a swap underneath. Telling one leg strike from another would need blow-by-blow data that this source simply does not record.

04 · striking distribution

Where strikes land, and how that changed

Measured per minute of cage time, MMA reorganised itself around striking from a distance. What it did not do is abandon anything else.

Striking at range rose more than sevenfold, from 0.86 to 6.50 landed strikes a minute. Over the same three decades the clinch went from 0.68 to 0.79, and ground striking from 1.22 to 0.76.

Wrestling did not leave the sport either; it changed jobs. Takedown attempts per minute rose 1.42×, stepping up once when the Unified Rules came in and then holding flat for twenty-two years. Accuracy went the other way in that same step, from 0.51 to 0.41, drifting on to 0.36 — fighters shoot more often now against opponents far better at stopping them.

Nothing emptied out. Range striking simply grew until it drowned everything else out.

05 · inside one fight

Round by round: Makhachev v Oliveira

One fight proves nothing about a model. What it can do is show you what a probability looks like from the inside — where a number like 73% actually comes from.

Makhachev v Oliveira — the real rounds

UFC 280, October 2022. Bars extend toward the fighter who owned the stat.

official round statistics · takedowns R1: 2 – 0

The model gave Makhachev 73% before the fight, and the round-by-round record shows where that number was coming from: three-quarters of the opening round spent in control, takedowns 2–0, and a striking edge in both rounds.

A 73% forecast is meant to come in about three times in four. This was one of the three — and it arrived for the reasons the model had already pointed at, which is the part that matters.

06 · era signatures

The shape of a three-round fight

Strip a fight back to nothing but how much work was done in each of its three rounds, and the decade is still legible. Nineties fights open at full tilt and fade. Fights from the 2000s hold a steady level. Modern fights build, saving their busiest work for the final round.

For the modern eras the shapes are far enough apart to be more than noise, with up to 1,482 fights behind each one.

07 · evolution of the sport

Finish rate and pace

Two trends that ought to pull against each other, and don’t. Fighters throw roughly three times as much per round as they used to, and fights are far less likely to end early.

Finish rate against striking pace

Share of fights ending early, against significant strikes landed per fighter per round. Dashed verticals are rule changes — gloves 1997, Unified Rules 2001.

n = 8,678 method-classified fights · round-1 share of finishes 77% → 49%

The explanation is that the fighters caught up with each other. Early MMA was mostly specialists meeting people who had no answer to them — 93% of nineties finishes came in the first round — and a mismatch that severe sorts itself out quickly. Today’s fighters are competent everywhere, so fights last longer even as the work rate climbs.

More violence per round, and fewer rounds ended by it, is what a sport looks like once everyone in it is good.

One caveat, stated plainly

How judges score a round also changed over this period. So some of the rise in decisions reflects a shift in scoring culture, not purely fighters becoming harder to finish.

Techniques spread and fade like anything else that catches on. Measured per minute of cage time across the same three decades, these three went in three entirely different directions.

09 · physical advantages

What physical advantages are worth

You cannot compare years against inches directly. So each advantage is first converted into the same unit — how unusual it is for that weight class — and then all of them are measured together, which stops any one from taking credit for another’s work.

Every physical advantage, on one scale

The age cliff

Win rate by age band, counting every fighter in every fight once. This is a cliff, not a gentle slope — and the sport is walking towards the edge of it, with the average age up from 29.7 to 31.9 since 1994.

being younger wins 56.9% · n = 8,377 fights

Once both sit on the same scale, being the younger fighter is worth roughly three and a half times as much as being the longer one. Height cannot be separated from no effect at all. Fighting southpaw lands between the two at +0.145 — a real edge, and a smaller one than its reputation suggests.

One more result from the same work. Fighters who win by submission control 39.6% of the fight, against 28.6% for those who win on the judges’ cards — a gap of 11.0 points, and a firm one, sitting between +9.4 and +12.6. A submission is not a bolt from the blue. It is a siege that happens to end early.

These are associations, not causes. Younger fighters also tend to be matched differently, and that is baked into the numbers. The ranges shown are deliberately cautious: they are built by resampling fighters rather than fights, so one busy career cannot make a result look steadier than it is. That widens every interval by 1.04–1.17×.

10 · pre-finish indicators

The round before a finish

The round before a fight is stopped looks measurably different from the same round in a fight that goes on to the judges. Better still, the two ways of ending leave different marks — so the warning tells you not just that something is coming, but what.

85.6% of fighters who get knocked down even once, in any round, go on to lose the fight (n = 3,184)

A knockdown is the loudest thing that can happen inside a round without ending it, and on its own it very nearly settles the result. The more useful finding sits underneath: knockouts are announced by damage taken, submissions by ground given away.

That difference is what makes the warning worth having. A round where a fighter is stuck underneath someone, fending off submission attempts, looks nothing like a round where they are standing up and eating shots to the head — even though both can end the same way a few minutes later.

Finishes also bunch up at the ends of rounds: a quarter of them land in a round’s final minute, against 14% in its first. One caveat there — referees and corners make their own decisions at the end of a round, so not all of that is the fighters.

11 · career profiles

Reading a career year by year

Cut a tree open and its years are all there in order — thick rings for the good seasons, thin ones for the hard years, scars where something happened. A fighting career laid out year by year has exactly the same structure, so it can be drawn the same way.

The wide early rings are the climb. The notch in 2007 is the Serra knockout, the four blank rings are the years away from the sport, and the last ring, in 2017, is the comeback — a title in a second division, then retirement while still on top.

Data and methods

How these numbers were made

All computed figures derive from UFCStats.com public fight records (snapshot 2 August 2026): 8,794 fights, 41,392 fighter-round stat lines, 4,578 fighter profiles, 1994 to July 2026.

Method notes

  • 116 fights left out of the how-it-ended analyses — overturned results, disqualifications and no-contests
  • Duration and pace figures use standard five-minute-round formats only
  • Control time is only fully recorded from 2000 onward; earlier fights are treated as unknown, never as zero
  • Fighter details matched by name at 99.8% · eras grouped 1994–99 through 2020–26
  • Everything here is observed, not experimental. Matchmaking, judging culture and how quickly referees stop fights all shifted over this period, and all three sit behind these numbers

Results that came back flat

Results that came back flat, and got published anyway. A flat result is still a finding about the sport, and each of these settles an argument people have a lot.

  • Leg strikes have not increased — flat at 15–17% for two decades. The calf-kick era was a swap, not a surge
  • Heavyweights are no more unpredictable round to round than lighter divisions: 6.36 against 6.40
  • Nobody fades in the championship rounds — round-5 output of 19.3 actually beats round 3 at 18.3
  • Reach does buy control, measured inside individual fights: −0.0175 of control share per typical reach advantage (range −0.026 to −0.009)

Next findings

Each one comes with the test that would kill it, written down in advance. A finding you cannot prove wrong is not a finding — it is a slogan.

Fighter cross-dating

Work out which decade a fight came from using nothing but its round-by-round numbers — the way you can date a wooden beam from its tree rings.

Kill test: if it cannot beat simply guessing the most common era, then the era signature is a trick of averaging rather than something real in the fights themselves.

Style-drift alerts

Flag fighters whose recent performances have drifted away from their own long-run habits — not from the divisional average, but from who they used to be.

Kill test: a flag ought to arrive before results change. If flagged fighters keep winning at their usual rate, the alert is measuring noise and nothing else.

Round-1 dominance index

Score every fight’s opening round on a single scale, then ask whether watching round one tells you anything the pre-fight forecast did not already know.

Kill test: if it does not beat the model’s own pre-fight line, round one is adding nothing the features had not already accounted for.

Fight sonification

Turn a fight’s round-by-round record into sound — pace becomes tempo, damage becomes volume, control becomes the notes that hold.

Kill test: this is presentation, not evidence, and it will be labelled that way. If listeners cannot pick the era or the finish better than chance, it stays an illustration and is never cited as a result.
