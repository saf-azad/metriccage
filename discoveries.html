Findings

Every number below is computed, not quoted — from the public round-by-round record of every UFC fight since 1993. Each figure carries the sample it was measured on, and each section states the test that would falsify it.

01 · method

Rates, not shares

Fight duration and total output both rose substantially across this window, so every era figure on this page is expressed per minute of elapsed cage time rather than as a share of a moving total.

A share has two moving parts: the behaviour being measured, and the size of the pool it is a share of. When the pool roughly triples over three decades, a share can fall while the thing it measures holds steady — so shares and rates answer different questions, and only one of them is about fighting.

Measuring against the clock removes the ambiguity. Combined significant strikes landed per fight-minute go from 2.76 to 8.04 across the window, which is the scale of the change the denominator has to absorb.

Compute combined significant strikes landed per fight-minute by era. If it were flat, shares and rates would agree and this control would be unnecessary. It goes 2.76 → 8.04.

03 · target selection

Leg strikes by era

Leg-strike share has held flat at 15–17% for two decades, moving from 0.147 to 0.162 across the full window.

The calf kick arrived and spread in that period, and this is what its arrival looks like in aggregate: a substitution rather than an addition. Fighters swapped which leg strike they threw without changing how often they threw one.

A useful reminder that aggregate counts hide substitutions — per-technique granularity needs play-by-play data this source does not carry.

04 · striking distribution

Where strikes land

Measured per minute of cage time, the sport reorganised around range striking — and it did so by growth rather than by displacement.

Distance striking rose more than sevenfold, from 0.86 to 6.50 landed strikes per minute. Over the same period the clinch went from 0.68 to 0.79 and the ground from 1.22 to 0.76.

Wrestling did not leave the sport; it changed jobs. Takedown attempts rose 1.42× per minute, stepping up once at the Unified Rules and then holding flat for twenty-two years, while takedown accuracy moved from 0.51 to 0.41 in that same step and drifted to 0.36.

Nothing emptied out. Distance striking grew until it out-voted everything else.

The model gave Makhachev 73% before the fight, and the round data shows why the number was set where it was: 76% of the opening round spent in control, a takedown count of 2–0, and a striking edge held in both rounds.

07 · evolution of the sport

Finish rate and pace

Two series that would not normally be expected to move together: fights got longer while output per round tripled.

Finish rate against striking pace

Share of fights ending early, against significant strikes landed per fighter per round. Dashed verticals are rule changes — gloves 1997, Unified Rules 2001.

n = 8,678 method-classified fights · round-1 share of finishes 77% → 49%

The explanation is skill symmetry. Early MMA rewarded specialists meeting non-specialists — 93% of nineties finishes came in round one — and a mismatch resolves quickly. Modern fighters are complete across all phases, so fights last longer even as output per round rises.

More violence per round, and fewer rounds ended by it, is what a maturing competitive field looks like.

Confound named

Judging criteria changed across this window too, so some of the growth in decisions reflects scoring culture rather than skill parity alone.

The age cliff

Win rate by age band, every fighter-fight counted once. A cliff rather than a slope — and the sport is walking up it, mean age 29.7 → 31.9 since 1994.

being younger wins 56.9% · n = 8,377 fights

Youth carries roughly three and a half times the weight of reach once both are on a common scale, and height does not separate from zero at all. Southpaw stance sits between them at +0.145.

A related result from the same fits: submission winners control 39.6% of elapsed fight time against 28.6% for decision winners, a gap of 11.0 points with a confidence interval of +9.4 to +12.6. Submissions are sieges that happen to end early.

These are associations, not causes — age gaps correlate with matchmaking. Intervals resample fighters rather than fights, which widens them 1.04–1.17×.

10 · pre-finish indicators

The round before a finish

The round preceding a stoppage looks measurably different from the same round in a fight that goes the distance, and the two ways of ending leave distinct traces.

85.6% of fighters who suffer a single knockdown — in any round — go on to lose the fight (n = 3,184)

A knockdown is the loudest thing that can happen inside a round without ending it, and on its own it is close to decisive. The more useful result is the split: knockouts are preceded by damage absorbed, submissions by position surrendered.

That distinction is what makes the signal usable. A round in which a fighter concedes control and defends submission attempts looks nothing like a round in which they absorb head strikes, even though both can end the same way.

Fights also cluster at the ends of rounds: a quarter of all finishes land in a round’s final minute, against 14% in its first. Confound named — referees and corners also act at round ends.

Data and methods

How these numbers were made

All computed figures derive from UFCStats.com public fight records (snapshot 2 August 2026): 8,794 fights, 41,392 fighter-round stat lines, 4,578 fighter profiles, 1994 to July 2026.

Method notes

  • 116 fights excluded from method analyses (overturned results, DQs, no-contests)
  • Duration and pace restricted to standard 5-minute-round formats
  • Control time fully tracked from 2000; earlier records treated as missing, never as zero
  • Attributes joined by name at 99.8% match · era buckets 1994–99 through 2020–26
  • All observational: matchmaking, judging culture and stoppage norms are named confounds

Further results

Measurements that came back flat. A flat result is a finding about the sport, and each of these settles a question that gets argued a lot.

  • Leg-strike share is flat at 15–17% across two decades — the calf-kick era is a substitution, not an increase
  • Heavyweights are no more volatile round to round than lighter classes: 6.36 against 6.40
  • Fighters surge in championship rounds — round-5 output of 19.3 exceeds round 3 at 18.3
  • Reach does buy control, measured within fights: −0.0175 control share per SD (CI −0.026 to −0.009)
