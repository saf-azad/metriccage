Every number below is computed, not quoted — from the public round-by-round record of every UFC fight since 1993. In August 2026 all of them were recomputed against elapsed fight time, because the denominator roughly tripled across this window, and four published findings did not survive it.

Discovery 00 · the correction that reframes the rest

A share of a tripling total
is not a trend.

Every rate on this page has a denominator, and for two decades that denominator moved. Fights lasted longer, went to more rounds, and the total volume of recorded striking climbed with them. Any quantity expressed as a share of that total is therefore measuring two things at once: the behaviour itself, and the size of the pool it is a share of.

Divide by elapsed cage time instead and the two come apart — and four findings this site had published did not survive the change.

Falsify it: compute combined significant strikes landed per fight-minute by era. If it were flat, shares and rates would agree. It goes 2.76 → 8.04.

Per minute of actual cage time, 1990s → 2020s: distance 0.86 → 6.50 · clinch 0.68 → 0.79 · ground 1.22 → 0.76. The ground did decline — by 38%, not 79% — and the clinch, listed as dying, is 16% busier than in 1997. Distance striking grew more than sevenfold and out-voted everything else.

Discovery 00b · the chart almost nobody publishes

How thin is the evidence, year by year?

The corpus is not spread evenly across the years it covers. Reading a trend from 1994 to 2026 means reading a few hundred rounds at one end and tens of thousands at the other — and a chart that draws both at equal visual weight quietly implies they carry equal evidential weight.

They do not. Every era claim on this page now prints the n for its own cell rather than the corpus total.

Discovery 00c · a null worth more than most findings

The calf-kick era
does not exist in the data.

The calf kick genuinely did arrive, and you can watch it arrive. What it did not do is change how often fighters kicked legs at all — because it largely replaced the low kicks they were already throwing.

The boom was real but it was a substitution — calf instead of thigh — invisible in aggregate counts. Aggregates hide substitutions.

Discovery 01 · fight sequences

Distance striking swallowed the sport.

Distance striking did not win by making the other positions rarer. It won by growing enormously while they stayed roughly where they had always been — which is why the share view read as a collapse and the rate view reads as an expansion.

Wrestling did not disappear; it changed jobs. Takedown attempts rose 1.42× per minute — not the 2.35× the per-fight count implies — one step at the Unified Rules, then twenty-two flat years. Takedown accuracy: 0.51 → 0.41 in one move, then a slow drift to 0.36.

Read together, the corrected finding is bigger than the one it replaces: nothing emptied out and nothing died — distance striking grew more than sevenfold and out-voted everything else.

Discovery 02 · inside one fight

Round one said what 73% couldn’t.

Makhachev v Oliveira — the real rounds

UFC 280, October 2022. Bars extend toward the fighter who owned the stat.

official round statistics · takedowns R1: 2 – 0

A single fight cannot validate a model, and this one is not offered as validation. What it can do is show what a probability looks like from the inside — the round-level record of a fight the model called correctly, and the reason it was never especially close.

The model gave Makhachev 73% before the fight. A 73% is supposed to lose about once in four — the round data shows this was the three-in-four, arriving on schedule. That is what calibration means.

Next test: score every fight’s rounds this way and check whether round-1 dominance predicts finishes better than the pre-fight line alone.

Discovery 03 · prove it yourself

You can carbon-date a fight from three bars.

Strip a fight down to how much work was done in each of its three rounds and the era is still legible. The shape alone carries it: nineties fights open at full output and fall away, 2000s fights hold a level, and modern fights build.

The era arcs differ beyond noise for the modern buckets (n up to 1,482 per era). The 1990s arc rests on n = 3 — because 85% of nineties fights never reached a decision, which is itself the era’s signature.

Discovery 04 · evolution of the sport

Finishes halved. Violence didn’t.

Two curves that should not coexist

Share of fights ending early, against significant strikes landed per fighter per round. Dashed verticals are rule changes — gloves 1997, Unified Rules 2001.

n = 8,678 method-classified fights · round-1 share of finishes fell 77% → 49%

Two things happened at once that ought to pull against each other. Fights stopped ending early — the share reaching a decision roughly doubled — and at the same time fighters started throwing far more. More violence per round, and fewer rounds ended by it.

The resolution of the paradox is skill symmetry: early MMA was mismatch harvesting — 93% of nineties finishes came in round one. Modern fighters are complete, so fights last longer at far higher output.

Confound kept honest: judging criteria changed too — some of the decision growth is scoring culture, not just skill parity.

The age cliff

Win rate by age band, every fighter-fight counted once. A cliff, not a slope — and the sport is walking up it (mean age 29.7 → 31.9 since 1994).

being younger wins 56.9% · n = 8,377 fights

And one reversal: submission winners control more of the fight, not less — 39.6% of elapsed time against 28.6% for decision winners (+11.0 points, CI +9.4 to +12.6). The raw-seconds version was settled by arithmetic before anyone threw a punch. Submissions are sieges that end early.

These are associations, not causes — age gaps correlate with matchmaking. Intervals resample fighters, not fights; that widens them 1.04–1.17×, less than you might guess.

Discovery 07 · the pre-finish fingerprint

The round before the end has a fingerprint.

85.6% of fighters who suffer a single knockdown — in any round — go on to lose the fight (n = 3,184)

A knockdown is the loudest thing that can happen inside a round without ending it, and it turns out to be close to decisive on its own. But the more useful result is that the two ways a fight can end leave different traces in the round before: knockouts are preceded by damage absorbed, submissions by position surrendered.

Fights die at the ends of rounds: a quarter of all finishes land in a round’s final minute, against 14% in a round’s first. Confound kept honest: referees and corners also act at round ends.

Data & methods

How these numbers were made.

All computed figures derive from UFCStats.com public fight records (snapshot 2 August 2026): 8,794 fights, 41,392 fighter-round stat lines, 4,578 fighter profiles, 1994 → July 2026.

Method notes

  • 116 fights dropped from method analyses (overturned results, DQs, no-contests)
  • Duration and pace restricted to standard 5-minute-round formats
  • Control time fully tracked only from 2000 — earlier treated as missing, never zero
  • Attributes joined by name (99.8% match) · era buckets 1994–99 … 2020–26
  • All observational: matchmaking, judging culture and stoppage norms are named confounds

Nulls we kept

Findings that didn’t survive contact with the data, reported instead of deleted — a results page that only shows hits is advertising.

  • Long arms mean long control — refuted at career level; the within-fight test reversed it (see the ledger)
  • The calf-kick boom shows in target mix — flat at 15–17% for two decades
  • Heavyweights are wilder round to round — volatility 6.36 v 6.40; the chaos is evenly distributed
  • Championship rounds are where fighters fade — round-5 output (19.3) beats round 3 (18.3); fighters surge

The queue

Same engine, next discoveries.

Each one ships with the test that would kill it — a finding you can’t falsify is a slogan.

Fighter cross-dating

Predict a fight’s era from its round-by-round line alone, the way a dendrochronologist dates a beam from its rings.

Kill test: if the classifier cannot beat the era base rate on held-out fights, the era signature is an artefact of aggregation rather than a property of the fights.

Style-drift alerts

Flag fighters whose recent output has departed from their own established career profile, rather than from the divisional average.

Kill test: a drift flag should precede a change in results. If flagged fighters go on winning at their usual rate, the alert is measuring noise.

Round-1 dominance index

Score every fight’s opening round on the same scale, then ask whether it predicts a finish over and above the pre-fight probability.

Kill test: no improvement in log-loss against the model’s own line means round one adds nothing the features already carry.

Fight sonification

Render a fight’s round-by-round record as audio — pace as tempo, damage as dynamics, control as sustain.

Kill test: this is a presentation, not a finding. If listeners cannot pick the era or the finish above chance, it stays labelled illustration and is never cited as evidence.
