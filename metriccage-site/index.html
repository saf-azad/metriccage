The premise

Accuracy is the wrong target.

A model that says 70% and is right 70% of the time is doing its job. A model that says 90% and is wrong half the time it is confident is worse than useless — it is confidently misleading.

Most fight-prediction projects optimise for the headline: what percentage of winners did it call? That number is easy to inflate and almost impossible to act on. Picking the betting favourite in every UFC bout already lands somewhere near 60%, so an accuracy score in that range tells you nothing about whether a model has learned anything at all.

Calibration asks a harder and more useful question: when this system says 65%, does the fighter actually win about 65 times in a hundred? That property is what makes a probability composable — you can size a position on it, compare it against a market line, or stack it with other information. A pick cannot do any of that.

So MetricCage optimises log-loss and Brier score, not accuracy — and reports accuracy only as context, sitting next to the baseline it has to beat.

Every design decision here exists to stop the model from seeing the future.

Why the temporal split matters more than it sounds. A random train/test split on fight data leaks in two directions at once: a fighter's later bouts inform their earlier feature values, and the test set contains fights whose participants the model has already watched mature. Both inflate scores and neither survives contact with a real upcoming card. Splitting strictly by date — and testing the assumption with dedicated leakage tests — is the only way the numbers further down mean anything.

Results

The honest scoreboard.

Measured on 695 fights the model had never seen, from years it was never trained on — and every number now carries the interval it was always entitled to. That matters in both directions: the AUC target sits outside the interval, so the miss is real rather than noise, while the Brier target sits inside it, so “near” is the honest word and there is now a reason for it.

The gap is discrimination, not calibration — and that is now an identity, not a claim. Decomposing the Brier score gives 0.2286 = reliability 0.0033 − resolution 0.0240 + uncertainty 0.2496. Reliability near zero is the calibration half. Resolution of 0.024 against a ceiling of 0.250 is the discrimination half. The model’s probabilities are honest; it simply cannot spread them far apart, which is why the roadmap targets features rather than recalibration. One correction to the older version of this paragraph: the site used to quote ECE 0.011, a figure measured on the superseded three-stream model. Re-measured on the bundle actually shipping, it is 0.049 — still inside the target, no longer “near-perfect”.

What drives a prediction

Style matchup outranks raw output.

This was not the expected result. Striking volume, Elo and age differential are the features every fight model reaches for first, and all three sit below a pair of unsupervised cluster memberships derived from a Gaussian mixture over fighting-style statistics.

The interpretation is the interesting part: the model is telling us that who a fighter is stylistically — pressure striker, distance counter-puncher, wrestler, submission hunter — predicts outcomes better than how much of anything they do. That matches how the sport is actually discussed and rarely how it is actually modelled.

It also explains the sparse-fighter weakness documented further down. If the strongest feature is a cluster assignment, then a fighter with too few bouts to cluster confidently is a fighter the model is quietly guessing about.
