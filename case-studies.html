This page used to say the model went 9 for 13. It did not: of the thirteen bouts, one was a no-contest and four were UFC debutants the model cannot score at all, so five of those nine picks were market-led. On the eight it actually scored it went five — against an expected 5.33 from its own probabilities (central 80%: 4 to 7).

Which makes the card, statistically, a non-event. Five against an expected 5.33 is the middle of the distribution; the model did roughly what it said it would do, and the sense that something had gone badly wrong came entirely from counting bouts it was never in a position to forecast.

The real failure was the accounting, not the forecast. Nine of thirteen was a number produced by quietly crediting the model for picks that were really the betting market’s, and the write-up that followed then explained a gap that had never opened. The correction cost the project its most-quoted case study.

What replaced it is worth more. Asking why so many bouts were unscoreable led to a population split across the whole blind test — and there the effect is unmistakable, large, and measured on 297 fights rather than four.

Lesson. A calibrated model can still be confidently wrong about a population it has never seen — calibration is measured in aggregate; coverage gaps hide inside it.

Method

How these were scored.

A card is only evidence if the forecast existed before the fights did. Each set of predictions is generated and stored ahead of the event, using the feature matrix as it stood at that moment — no post-hoc feature updates, no re-running with hindsight, no quietly dropping a bout that turned out badly.

Bouts the model cannot score are marked unscoreable rather than filled in. A UFC debutant has no history in the feature matrix, so any number attached to them would be the market’s opinion wearing the model’s name — which is precisely the mistake the Macau entry above was built on.

A pick counts as correct when the fighter given the higher win probability wins. No-contests and overturned results are excluded from the denominator, not counted as losses.

Card records are raw counts, never percentages. Nine out of thirteen is nine out of thirteen; it is not “69% accuracy”, and treating it as such is exactly the overclaiming this project is built to avoid.

Thirteen fights cannot support a percentage to two significant figures. The interval around it is wide enough to contain almost any story you would like to tell.

The aggregate metrics on the results scoreboard — computed across 695 fights — are the numbers that carry statistical weight. These cards are here for what they reveal about failure modes.
