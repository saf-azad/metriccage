Architecture · features · evidence

Inside the model

There is no single model. There are four, and they disagree with each other on purpose. Underneath them, every fight is reduced to a row of 102 numbers, and each family of numbers is tested against outcomes rather than taken on faith.

Ensemble weights were fitted on a 2023 validation slice by minimising log loss, not chosen by hand. The blend passes through an isotonic calibrator that maps raw output onto observed frequencies, then a temperature adjustment that pulls extreme calls back toward the middle. Both exist because raw ensembles are systematically overconfident.

The Elo underneath is not chess Elo.

It differs in three ways: the K-factor scales down as a fighter accumulates bouts, so a debutant’s rating moves faster than a veteran’s; finishes update ratings more than decisions, with knockouts at 1.15 times the base and submissions at 1.10; and a fighter idle for more than a year is regressed toward the mean, capped at a quarter of the way back. Elo difference and Elo-implied probability sit fifth and sixth in the boosted model’s importance ranking.

The 102 features

Eleven families, each tested against outcomes

Most features are differentials, computed as fighter A minus fighter B, so a positive value favours A. Rate statistics are decayed with a 365-day half-life, meaning a bout from a year ago counts half as much as a recent one and a bout from three years ago counts about an eighth.

What follows describes what each family measures and whether it actually works. That last part matters, because a feature that sounds clever and carries no signal is worse than no feature at all.

A note on the evidence below.

Each response curve splits every UFC bout from 2015 onward (5,522 fights) into five equal groups by the feature in question and reports the observed outcome rate in each. This is descriptive rather than causal: features correlate with each other, so these curves show what a feature carries, not what it independently contributes.

Trajectory is the strongest curve in the entire feature set: 40.3 per cent win rate in the bottom group rising to 60.7 in the top, a twenty-point spread. It exists because a fighter’s record describes where they have been rather than where they are going: an ascending-trajectory score weights recent results with a 200-day half-life and adjusts for opponent quality.

Net striking differential is the third most important feature in the whole model, and striking defence behaves cleanly: fighters in the top group by defence differential win 59.7 per cent of the time against 43.4 in the bottom. Control time is weaker than most people expect, moving win rate only from 46.3 to 53.4 per cent across its range.

Reach is real but modest: giving away more than three inches drops win rate to 45.3 per cent, holding more than three inches raises it to 53.8. A six-point swing at the extremes of a range most fights never reach, roughly the right size for something the sport treats as decisive.

The rust flag earns its rarity.

A specific flag fires when the favourite is the rusty one. It happens on only 77 fights, but when it does, the favourite’s win rate falls from 49.5 to 39.0 per cent. That is what a flag should do: fire rarely and mean something when it fires.

Knockout threat against chin durability

Power as a property of the pairing

Rather than treating power as a property of a fighter, this family treats it as a property of a pairing. Knockout danger runs against the specific opponent’s chin, which combines knockdowns absorbed, strikes absorbed per minute, a knockout-fragility term, and an accumulated-trauma penalty that grows with career knockout losses.

The knockout family against its baselines

Dashed markers are the base rates. Hover a bar for the comparison.

Baselines: A wins 49.4% · 31.4% of fights end by KO

The chin-vulnerable flag is the strongest single binary signal in the feature set. When it fires, the knockout rate is thirteen points above the base rate. It fires rarely, on about five per cent of fights, which is what a flag should do.

Knockout danger itself is built as half a knockout-per-round rate, three-tenths a knockdown bonus, and two-tenths career finish rate. The differential moves fighter A’s win rate from 40.9 per cent in the bottom group to 57.8 in the top.

Submission chains

Reasoning about position without positional data

The most elaborate piece of feature engineering in the model, and its 2026 performance is the most instructive thing in the season report.

The design does not use submission attempts as a rate. It reasons about position. Each fighter’s archetype vector is converted into an estimated distribution over eight grappling positions, and each position carries a base submission probability derived from historical rates. A fighter with a high grappling-first score is estimated to spend up to 40 per cent of ground time in back control and very little standing; a pure striker sits at the opposite end.

Three adjustments follow. A chain-weight factor of up to 1.4 rewards fighters who string attempts together rather than hunting a single hold. A round-fatigue multiplier scales the danger upward from 1.00 in round one to 1.25 in round five, because tired fighters get caught. And the whole thing is gated by whether the fight reaches the ground at all, modulated by the opponent’s takedown defence.

Across 2015 to 2025 the feature did exactly what it was built to do: bottom quartile of chain danger, 11.6 per cent of fights ended by submission; top quartile, 22.6. Close to a doubling, with the model’s own submission probability tracking the feature at a correlation of 0.81. Then the 2026 season arrived and the relationship evaporated; the collapse is charted in full on the discoveries page.

Stance geometry

The southpaw edge, amplified by style

Open stance (orthodox against southpaw) occurs in 27.6 per cent of bouts since 2015, and the southpaw wins 52.6 per cent of those. A real but small edge. The interesting part is that the edge is not uniform, and the model encodes why.

Stance multiplier by archetype

How much each style depends on angles and geometry. Hover for the value.

Adjusted for how rare southpaws are in each division

A southpaw kickboxer gets close to the full benefit of the open-stance geometry. A southpaw wrestler gets very little, because the fight he wants does not happen at kicking range.

The data supports the design. Splitting open-stance fights by amplified score, the southpaw wins 49.0 per cent in the weakest quartile and 55.1 per cent in the third. In other words, the naive version of this feature, a simple binary, would be averaging a genuine six-point edge together with nothing at all.

The amplified score converts into probability directly.

An estimated win-probability uplift of 6.5 per cent per unit of amplified score, further adjusted for how rare southpaws are in that division, since the advantage comes partly from unfamiliarity. Open-stance fights are also slightly more likely to end early: 51.5 per cent against 49.5.

Learned style clusters

The clusters nobody designed

The family the model leans on hardest was not designed by hand at all. A Gaussian mixture model with seven components is fitted to fighter statistic vectors from the training period only, with the number of components chosen by information criterion and sparse fighters shrunk toward their division prior. Each fighter gets seven soft membership probabilities that sum to one.

These clusters are the two highest-gain features in the boosted model by a wide margin. Cluster 2 membership for both fighters carries more than twice the importance of net striking, which is the best of the hand-built features.

The model has found something none of the eight hand-defined archetypes captures, and nobody can say precisely what it is.

That is worth stating plainly rather than dressing up. A hand-built feature can be argued about; a learned cluster can only be measured.

Negative results

Two features that do not earn their place

Not everything works, and a report that only lists the wins is not worth much.

Pace mismatch is close to inert

Across its full range the win rate moves between 47.4 and 50.7 per cent, and the finish rate barely responds either. The idea that a tempo gap should let the faster fighter dictate does not survive contact with the data. Archetype distance and similarity are similarly flat as win predictors, though distance shows a mild U-shape against finishing.

Prime mismatch should be checked

It ranks seventh by importance, yet it is a binary that fires on only 8.1 per cent of fights. High gain on a rare flag is a pattern that usually means the boosted model has found a narrow pocket of the data to memorise. It may be genuine. It should be checked.

Discipline

The rule that keeps it honest

Every feature for a fight on a given date is computed using only data from strictly before that date. This sounds obvious and is very easy to get wrong. A career average that quietly includes the fight being predicted will produce beautiful accuracy and no predictive power whatsoever.

The codebase enforces this with regression tests.

They check that a fight’s feature row is bit-identical whether or not later fights are present in the input. If tomorrow’s results can change today’s features, the test fails.

One further feature exists in the codebase but is switched off in the deployed model: implied_prob_a, the vig-removed closing-line probability. Every number on this site comes from the no-odds model. Adding the market is the single largest available gain in discrimination, and also the point at which the model stops being independent of the betting line.
