Model evidence

This is the working behind the front page. Every chart here comes from the shipped model itself, re-scored on the same fixed split, and each one names the file it was computed from — so any figure can be followed back to the script that produced it.

The material below gets technical. Each section opens with the question it is answering in plain terms, then the number that answers it.

01

Where the score comes from

A single score tells you how well a forecast did, but not why. Murphy’s decomposition breaks it into three parts — two the model can influence, and one it never will.

The three parts, in plain terms

  • Reliability — are the stated odds honest? This is the dishonesty term, so lower is better and zero is perfect.
  • Resolution — does the model commit, or hedge everything near 50/50? Here higher is better, which is why it is subtracted.
  • Uncertainty — how hard the sport is to call in the first place. No model touches this one. It is the same number whoever is forecasting.

Brier 0.2286 = reliability 0.0033 − resolution 0.0240 + uncertainty 0.2496 · that is an improvement of +0.084 on simply quoting the historical average every time

The three parts, drawn to scale

Reliability sits at 0.0033 — all but invisible on this scale, which is exactly where it belongs. Uncertainty at 0.2496 dwarfs everything else, because most of what makes a fight hard to predict is simply that fights are hard to predict.

analysis/model_eval.json · murphy_quantile

How often it commits

Across all 695 fights the model reports only 24 different probabilities. The most common of them, 0.5621, covers 207 fights — a lot of a UFC card really is close to a coin toss, and the model says so rather than manufacturing confidence it does not have.

28% of forecasts sit outside the 30–70 band

Three methods were tried for the final honesty adjustment, and isotonic regression earned the job on the numbers: test log-loss 0.6463, against 0.6498 for Platt scaling and 0.6512 with no adjustment at all.

How the blend compares with its parts

Two questions decide whether blending four models was worth the trouble. Does the blend beat the best model inside it? And how much does each of the four contribute? The charts below answer them in that order.

Take one model away — how much worse does it get?

Each model is removed in turn and the blend rebuilt from what is left. Longer bars mark the models the blend leans on most.

XGBoost carries the most weight: losing it costs +0.0072 in log-loss. Logistic regression and the LSTM follow. Elo comes out roughly neutral, and not because it knows nothing — its ratings are already fed to the other three as a feature, so most of what it has to say has arrived before it votes.

03

What drives a prediction

The test is deliberately blunt. Take one feature, scramble its values across fights the model has never seen, and measure how much worse the forecasts get. Whatever hurts most to lose was doing the most work — and nothing passes this test on the strength of how promising it merely looked during training.

How much worse the forecasts get when a feature is scrambled

Measured on the 695-fight blind test set. Higher is more important.

analysis/model_eval2.json · permutation importance

Reading the ranking

Age difference leads at +0.049, pace mismatch follows at +0.038, and the two fighters’ UFC fight counts take third and fourth. Freshness, tempo, and depth of record — in that order.

Scrambling is the right test because it is done on fights the model has never seen. Tree-based models will report which features they leaned on, but that report comes from the data they were built on and flatters anything that can be sliced a lot of ways. It describes how the model was assembled, not what wins fights.

Both experience counts landing in the top four is precisely why performance rises with fighter history — measured directly in section 04.

04

Where the model is strong, and where it is not

The model knows a fighter only through their UFC record, which raises an obvious question: what happens when there is barely any record to read? Rather than assume, the same 695 fights are split by how much history each matchup carries.

Performance against available history

Same model, same test years — split by how much UFC history the matchup carries. Higher is better; 0.500 would mean guessing.

analysis/model_eval2.json · experience_auc

What this establishes

When both fighters have five or more UFC bouts behind them, the model reaches 0.718 — comfortably its best work, and the situation that covers most of a typical card. Where one fighter is close to a debutant, it drops to 0.603 and the model is honest about being on thinner ground.

The measurement also sets the next priority. Going after fighters’ pre-UFC records tops the roadmap precisely because this chart puts a number on what those records would be worth, instead of leaving it as a hunch.

Tapology and Sherdog are already being collected. That gap of 0.114 is the size of the prize.

05

How it holds up year to year

A model can look excellent overall and still be carried by one lucky season. Scoring each year separately shows whether performance is steady, and shading the years the model learned from lets the untouched ones be read on their own.

Score by year — the axis is anchored, so flat really is flat

Shaded spans are the years the model was built on; the unshaded years on the right it had never seen. The dashed line at 0.250 is what a coin flip scores.

validation 0.222 · test 0.229 · analysis/model_eval.json · brier_by_year

The tuning year comes in at 0.222 and the untouched test years at 0.229. A gap that small is the sign of a model that learned the sport rather than memorising the fights it was shown — the failure known as overfitting, and the one most easily hidden by a flattering headline number.

06

How to check all of it

Everything above comes from re-scoring the shipped four-model bundle on the same fixed split. The charts describe the model that actually runs, not a rebuilt approximation of it.

The split

Learn from 2005–2022, tune on 2023, test on 1 January 2024 to 4 May 2025 — 5,563 fights, then 504, then 695. The boundaries are fixed dates and do not move between runs.

The check

Re-scoring reproduces the bundle’s recorded results to 2.2×10⁻¹⁶ — machine precision, or as close to identical as a computer gets. Nothing on this page is an approximation.

The scripts

Three scripts — model_eval.py, model_eval2.py and score_streams.py — write out the numbers these charts read. Point them at a newer snapshot of the data and the figures regenerate themselves.
