The premise

Calibration over accuracy

A model that says 70% and is right about 70% of the time is doing its job. A model that says 90% and is wrong half the time it is confident is worse than useless — it is confidently misleading.

Most fight-prediction projects optimise for the headline: what share of winners did it call? That number is easy to inflate and almost impossible to act on. Picking the betting favourite in every UFC bout already lands near 60%, so an accuracy figure in that range says nothing about whether a model has learned anything.

Calibration asks the more useful question: when this system says 65%, does that fighter win about 65 times in a hundred? That property is what makes a probability composable. You can size a position on it, compare it against a market line, or stack it with other information. A pick can do none of those things.

MetricCage optimises log-loss and Brier score, both strictly proper scoring rules.

A strictly proper rule is minimised only when the reported number is the model’s honest belief, so there is nothing to gain by shading a forecast toward a safer-looking figure.

Why the temporal split matters more than it sounds

A random train/test split on fight data leaks in two directions at once. A fighter’s later bouts inform their earlier feature values, and the test set contains fights whose participants the model has already watched mature. Both inflate scores, and neither survives contact with a real upcoming card. Splitting strictly by date, and checking the assumption with dedicated leakage tests, is what makes every number below meaningful.

Train 2005–2022 · validate 2023 · test blind 2024–2025 — 5,563 / 504 / 695 fights. No shuffled split, no random K-fold.

Architecture

The ensemble

Given the same 102 features, four model families disagree in useful ways. Gradient boosting finds the interactions, logistic regression holds a stable linear baseline, the LSTM reads a career as an ordered sequence rather than a bag of averages, and Elo carries nothing but who beat whom.

Blending is worth doing because they fail in different places, and the blend beats every one of them individually.

Weights are not hand-set. They solve a constrained least-squares problem on the 2023 validation year, minimising log-loss with weights non-negative and summing to one.

Blending

Constrained least-squares over the four streams, fitted on the validation year alone so the test years stay untouched. Non-negativity keeps the result readable: no stream enters as a negative correction on another.

Calibration

Isotonic regression maps the blended score onto observed frequencies, then temperature scaling at T = 1.3 smooths the result. Isotonic reaches log-loss 0.6463, against 0.6498 for Platt scaling and 0.6512 uncalibrated.

Method of victory

A separate two-stage head predicts finish against decision, then submission against knockout. It is reported apart from the win probability so the two are never conflated.

The ensemble beats every one of its own components.

Test log-loss runs 0.6931 for an always-0.50 forecast, 0.6924 for the training base rate, then 0.6899 Elo, 0.6600 logistic regression, 0.6556 XGBoost and 0.6531 for the LSTM. The shipped blend reaches 0.6463 — ahead of its best single stream, which is the test an ensemble has to pass.

Where the score comes from

Decomposing the Brier score gives 0.2286 = reliability 0.0033 − resolution 0.0240 + uncertainty 0.2496. Reliability near zero is the calibration term, and it is where this model is strongest: the probabilities it reports are honest ones.

What drives a prediction

Feature importance

Importance is measured by permutation on held-out data: shuffle one feature on fights the model has never seen, and record how much worse the forecast gets. Nothing ranks highly here on the strength of how it looked during training.

Permutation importance — Δ log-loss when a feature is shuffled

Measured on the 695-fight blind test set. Higher is more important.

analysis/model_eval2.json · permutation importance

Age difference and pace mismatch lead, and the two fighters’ UFC fight counts follow immediately behind. That ordering matches how the sport is actually discussed: who is fresher, who sets the tempo, and who has been at this level long enough to have a measurable record.

It also says something useful about the model’s own shape. With both experience counts in the top four, the amount of UFC history available for a matchup is itself one of the strongest signals — which is why the system performs best on fighters it has seen most, and why extending fighter history is the leading item on the roadmap.

Permutation rather than gain

A tree model’s built-in gain is measured on the data the trees were fitted to, and rewards features that can be split many ways. Permutation on held-out fights answers the question that actually matters: does this feature help predict a fight nobody has seen yet?

How it is built

Engineering

Built as a system rather than a notebook. Every figure on this site traces back to a script and a stored output, and the whole pipeline re-runs from a fresh data snapshot.

Temporal integrity

Splits are by date, never by random seed, and dedicated leakage tests check the assumption rather than assuming it. Every feature is built from fights that had already finished at the time of the bout it describes.

Reproducibility

Re-scoring the shipped four-stream bundle on the frozen split reproduces its recorded test metrics to 2.2×10⁻¹⁶. The charts on the evidence page are the deployed model, not an approximation of it.

Provenance

Each chart names the file it was computed from. The analysis scripts emit JSON, the site reads the JSON, and no figure is typed in by hand along the way.

No runtime dependencies

Charts are hand-written SVG rather than a charting library, fonts are self-hosted, and there are no trackers or analytics. The site works offline and straight off the filesystem.
