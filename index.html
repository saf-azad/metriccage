The premise

Calibration over accuracy

Two words do most of the work on this site. Accuracy is how often a model names the winner. Calibration is whether the confidence attached to that name means anything at all.

A model that says 70% and is right about 70% of the time is doing its job. A model that says 90% and is wrong half the time it is that confident is worse than useless — it is confidently misleading.

Most fight-prediction projects chase the headline instead: what share of winners did it call? That number is easy to inflate and almost impossible to use. Backing the betting favourite in every UFC bout already lands near 60%, so an accuracy figure in that range tells you nothing about whether a model has learned anything.

Calibration asks the more useful question. When this system says 65%, does that fighter win about 65 times in a hundred? Get that right and the number becomes something you can act on: you can size a bet with it, hold it up against a bookmaker’s line, or fold it in with whatever else you know about the fight. A bare pick does none of that.

The model is trained to be honest rather than to look confident.

It is scored on log-loss and Brier score. Both belong to a family of measures with a useful property: the only way to score well is to report what you actually believe. Shading a forecast toward a safer-looking figure always costs you, so the model has no incentive to hedge — or to bluff.

Pipeline

Data pipeline

Every decision in this stage exists to stop the model from seeing the future. It is the least glamorous part of the project and the part everything else rests on.

01

Collect

Four separate collectors pull down results, round-by-round statistics, fighter details and event information. Where two of them cover the same ground, the records are checked against each other rather than merged and hoped for.

4 sources

02

Clean and reconcile

Names are what tie the four sources together, and names are the messiest thing in the data — spellings drift, nicknames come and go, fighters share surnames. Every fighter is resolved to a single identity, matching at 99.8%, before one feature is calculated.

one ID per fighter

03

Engineer

Career totals, recent form, pace, physical gaps and Elo rating — each one built strictly from fights that had already finished by the time the bout it describes was booked.

102 features

04

Split by date

Learn from the past, tune on the year after that, then test on years the model has never touched. The dividing line is always a date, never a shuffle.

walk-forward

Why splitting by date matters more than it sounds

Shuffling the fights at random and holding some back for testing sounds fair enough. It isn’t. Do it and the leak runs in two directions at once: a fighter’s later bouts quietly shape the numbers describing their earlier ones, and the held-back fights feature people the model has already watched grow into their prime. Both make the scores look better than they are, and neither survives contact with a card that has not happened yet. Splitting strictly by date — and running dedicated tests to check the split held — is what makes every number below worth reading.

Learn 2005–2022 · tune 2023 · test blind 2024–2025 — 5,563 / 504 / 695 fights. Nothing shuffled, and no fights held back at random.

Architecture

Four models, one answer

Hand the same 102 features to four different kinds of model and they disagree, usefully. Gradient boosting hunts for combinations — what happens when a fighter is both younger and busier. Logistic regression holds a plain, stable baseline that is hard to fool. The LSTM reads a career in order, so a record on the way up looks different from the same record on the way down. Elo knows nothing at all except who has beaten whom.

The reason to blend them is that they get things wrong in different places. Where one is lost, another is usually not — and the blend ends up ahead of all four on its own.

The weights are not set by hand. They are solved for on the 2023 validation year alone, chosen to make the blended forecast as honest as possible, kept non-negative, and made to sum to one.

How the weights are found

A constrained least-squares fit over the four models, run on the validation year only so the test years stay untouched. Holding every weight at zero or above keeps the result readable: no model enters the blend as a negative correction on another.

The final honesty adjustment

A blended score is not yet a probability. Isotonic regression pulls it onto the frequencies actually observed, so what gets reported is the rate at which scores of that size have really won, and temperature scaling at T = 1.3 smooths the edges. Isotonic won the job on the numbers: log-loss 0.6463, against 0.6498 for Platt scaling and 0.6512 with no adjustment at all.

How the fight ends

A separate model answers a separate question in two steps: finish or decision, and then submission or knockout. It is reported apart from the win probability, because knowing who wins and knowing how are not the same claim and should never be read as one.

Reading the table

The names are technical; the questions behind them are not.

  • AUC-ROC — can it tell winners from losers at all? A coin gets 0.500.
  • Brier score — how far the stated odds sat from what happened, on average. Lower is better; 0.250 is what saying “50/50” to everything earns you.
  • Log-loss — the same idea, but it punishes confident mistakes hardest. This is the number the model is trained to bring down.
  • ECE — the honesty check. Group the forecasts into ten bands and ask whether the 70% band really won about 70% of the time.
  • Accuracy — how often the fighter it favoured actually won. Reported for context only, for the reasons set out above.

The blend beats every model inside it.

Ranked by log-loss on the test years, lower being better: 0.6931 for always saying 50/50, 0.6924 for quoting the historical average, then 0.6899 for Elo, 0.6600 for logistic regression, 0.6556 for XGBoost and 0.6531 for the LSTM. The shipped blend lands at 0.6463 — ahead of its own best member, which is the one test a blend genuinely has to pass. A blend that cannot beat its best part is just an expensive way of running that part.

Where the score comes from

The Brier score can be split into three parts: 0.2286 = reliability 0.0033 − resolution 0.0240 + uncertainty 0.2496. Reliability measures dishonesty, so near zero is where you want it — and that is where this model is strongest. Uncertainty is the part nobody can touch: it is the sport being genuinely hard to call.

Feature importance

What drives a prediction

There is a simple way to find out what a piece of information is worth: take it away. Scramble one feature — age difference, say — across fights the model has never seen, then measure how much worse the forecasts get. Nothing earns a high rank here on the strength of how useful it merely looked during training.

How much worse the forecasts get when a feature is scrambled

Measured on the 695-fight blind test set. Higher is more important.

analysis/model_eval2.json · permutation importance

Age difference and pace mismatch lead, with the two fighters’ UFC fight counts close behind. That ordering is not far off how the sport gets talked about in a pub: who is fresher, who sets the tempo, and who has been at this level long enough to have a record worth reading.

The ranking also gives away something about the model itself. With both experience counts in the top four, the sheer amount of UFC history behind a matchup turns out to be one of the strongest signals available — which explains why the system does its best work on fighters it has watched the most, and why digging up more fighter history sits at the top of the roadmap.

Why scramble a feature instead of asking the model?

Tree-based models will happily report which features they leaned on, but that report is taken from the very data they were built on, and it flatters anything that can be sliced a lot of different ways. Scrambling a feature on fights nobody has seen yet answers the question that actually matters: does this help predict the next fight, or did it only ever help explain the last one?

How it is built

Engineering

This was built as a system, not a notebook. Every figure on the site traces back to a script and a saved output, and the whole pipeline runs again from scratch on a fresh snapshot of the data.

No peeking at the future

Fights are split by date, never at random, and dedicated tests check that the split actually held rather than trusting that it did. Every feature is built only from fights that had already finished when the bout it describes was made.

The same numbers, every time

Re-scoring the shipped four-model bundle on the frozen split reproduces its recorded test results to 2.2×10⁻¹⁶ — machine precision. What you see on the evidence page is the model itself, not a close relative of it.

Every figure has a source

Each chart names the file it was computed from. The analysis scripts write out the numbers, the site reads them, and nothing is typed in by hand anywhere along the way.

Nothing loads from anywhere else

The charts are hand-written rather than pulled from a charting library, the fonts are served from here, and there are no trackers or analytics of any kind. The site works offline, straight off a hard drive.
