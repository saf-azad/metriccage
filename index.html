The premise

Accuracy is the wrong target.

Pick the favourite in every fight and you will be right about 60% of the time. That number is free — it needs no features, no training and no model at all — and an accuracy score hands it back to you as though it were an achievement.

The deeper problem is that accuracy discards the only part of a forecast carrying any information. A 52% and a 93% are both simply “picks”; graded on accuracy they score identically. Yet they say completely different things about how much the model knows, and anyone acting on the number can tell them apart instantly. The metric cannot.

So MetricCage is graded on the probability rather than the label. Log-loss and Brier score are strictly proper: each is minimised only when the reported number is the model’s honest belief, which means there is no shading a forecast toward a safer-looking figure in order to flatter the score.

MetricCage optimises log-loss and Brier score, not accuracy — accuracy appears only as context, next to the ~60% favourite baseline it has to beat.

It still appears on the scoreboard, sitting at 59.6% — essentially level with that baseline. Leaving it out would look like hiding it.

Pipeline

From four scrapers to a leak-free feature matrix.

Collect

Four independent scrapers cover results, round-by-round statistics, fighter attributes and event metadata. Where they overlap they are cross-checked, not merged blindly.

Fighter names are the join key, and names are the messiest field in the source. Every fighter collapses to one canonical ID before a single feature is computed.

03

Engineer

Career aggregates, recent form, pace, physical differentials and Elo — each built strictly from fights that had already finished when the bout it describes was made.

102 features

04

Split temporally

Train on the past, tune on the year after, test on years the model has never touched. The boundary is a date, never a random seed.

walk-forward

Train 2005–2022 · validate 2023 · test blind 2024–2025 — 5,563 / 504 / 695 fights. No shuffled split, no random K-fold: a model that has seen the future is not a model.

The ensemble

Four models, one probability.

Given the same 102 features, four model families disagree in useful ways. Gradient boosting finds the interactions, logistic regression holds a stable linear baseline, the LSTM reads a career as an ordered sequence rather than a bag of averages, and Elo carries nothing but who beat whom. Blending is only worth doing because they fail in different places.

Weights solve a constrained least-squares problem on the 2023 validation year. A weight is an input, not a result — the leave-one-out ablation is what each stream is worth.

XGBoost35.3%
Logistic regression29.2%
Career LSTM23.1%
Elo12.4%

Blending. Constrained least-squares over the four streams, weights non-negative and summing to one.

Fitted on the 2023 validation year alone, so the test years stay untouched. Non-negativity keeps the result readable: no stream is allowed to enter as a negative correction on another.

Calibration. Isotonic regression, then temperature scaling at T = 1.3.

Isotonic maps the blended score onto observed frequencies; temperature scaling then softens the step function it produces. The cost is resolution — the fitted calibrator emits only 24 distinct values.

Method of victory. A separate two-stage head — finish v decision, then submission v knockout.

It is reported apart from the win probability because it performs considerably worse. Stage-two AUC is roughly 0.56, and a number that weak does not belong inside a headline figure.

Beyond prediction

What the data actually says.

Prediction is the engine. The findings are the payload — what falls out when you read fights as sequences and eras as populations.

Distance striking swallowed the sport.

Read as a share of landed strikes, the ground looked like it had collapsed. Measured against elapsed cage time it declined modestly — while distance striking grew more than sevenfold and out-voted everything else.

Per minute since the 1990s: distance +657% · clinch +16% · ground −38%

 Computed · n = 3,184 knockdowns

The round before the end has a fingerprint.

Fights announce themselves before they finish, and the two ways of ending announce themselves differently: knockouts warn through damage taken, submissions through territory conceded.

One knockdown, any round → 85.6% loss rate

Age beats reach — by 3.5×.

Years and inches are not a common unit, so the old version of this claim was really a statement about bin edges. Standardised within weight class and fitted jointly, the ranking survives — and height does not clear zero at all.

Where it breaks

Three things this model cannot currently do.

Every one of these is measured rather than described, and each has a line on the roadmap below. A limitation you can only phrase in prose is usually one you have not looked at closely enough.

The method head skews toward decisions

Predicting how a fight ends is a much harder problem than predicting who wins it, and the two-stage head does not hide that. Finishes are the rarer and noisier class, and the second stage barely clears chance.

Stage-two AUC is ≈ 0.56. It is reported separately from the win probability for exactly that reason.

No short-notice or weight-change feature

The feature matrix describes two fighters, not the circumstances they meet under. Nothing in the 102 features sees a bout taken on two weeks’ notice, a camp cut short, or a fighter moving up a division.

Exactly why it missed Pereira’s catchweight move — the model had no place to put the information.

UFC-only fight history

A fighter’s record begins the day they enter the UFC. Everything before it — regional titles, Contender Series, a decade abroad — is invisible, so a debutant looks identical to a genuine unknown.

Measured cost: AUC 0.718 → 0.603 when either fighter has fewer than three UFC bouts — the Macau card, converted into a number.
